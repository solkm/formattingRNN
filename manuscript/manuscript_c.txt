11   Abstract:
12   Neurons throughout the brain encode essentially everything, so why have distinct areas? We propose that
13   areas format different information sources differently, implying their computational role. We compared
14   the middle temporal area (MT), involved in visual motion perception, and the dorsolateral prefrontal
15   cortex (dlPFC), implicated in decision-making and reward. In a task that required combining motion and
16   reward information, both regions encoded both motion and reward, but formatted differently. MT
17   represented them separably, while dlPFC combined them in a decision-related format. A recurrent
18   network model (RNN) trained to reproduce that formatting predicted a distinct effect of stimulating those
19   areas. Consistent with predictions, MT stimulation biased choices while dlPFC stimulation produced
20   ‘winner-take-all’ outcomes. Our findings suggest that a modular structure flexibly reformats information
21   to enable complex behaviors.

23   Main Text:
24   Animals capable of complex behaviors tend to have more distinct brain areas than simpler organisms, and
25   artificial networks that perform many tasks tend to self-organize into modules (1-3). This suggests that
26   different brain areas serve distinct functions supporting complex behavior. Many experiments
27   demonstrate a functional segregation across different brain areas and pathways (4), yet the responses of
28   individual neurons reflect multiple sensory, cognitive, and/or motor processes. A recent set of studies
29   indicates that essentially anything that an animal senses, knows, or does can be decoded from neural
30   activity in any brain area (5-7).
31
32   The goal of our study was to understand what the representation of those information sources can tell us
33   about the role of a neuronal population in behavior. For example, in visual cortex, MT neurons are tuned
34   for visual motion direction (8-12), and their responses are modulated by reward expectation and other
35   cognitive processes (13-16). However, the presence of signals related to both motion and reward does
36   little to identify the role of these neurons in behavior. The same distributions of tuning and modulation are
37   consistent with multiple ways of formatting information in the population (which is sometimes called
38   representational geometry or neuronal population geometry (17, 18)). Neuronal population geometry
39   refers to the representation of stimulus, task, or motor information in a high dimensional space where the
40   response of each neuron represents one dimension (19-26). In such a space, neuronal population
41   responses to a circular variable like motion direction trace out a circle. Multiplicatively scaling the
42   responses of individual neurons to simulate, for example, attention or reward expectation (27, 28), can
43   stretch and/or shift that circle. A shift normal to the plane of the circle would mean that motion and
44   reward information are represented separably, while scaling the circle, or a shift in the plane of the circle,
45   would imply different formatting, in which motion and reward representations are intertwined.
46
47   The different possibilities arise because even identically tuned neurons have heterogeneous modulation by
48   cognitive processes (29). Simulating this heterogeneity by adding some randomness in the amount of
49   modulation by reward expectation among neurons with identical tuning for motion direction (Fig. 1A;
50   Methods) can produce population representations of motion direction and reward expectation that are
51   either separable (encoded in different dimensions in a space where the response of each neuron is one
52   dimension; Fig. 1B, C, D) or combined (encoded in the same dimensions; Fig. 1E, F, G). This simulation
53   demonstrates that the difference between separable and combined population formatting is not reliably
54   inferable from single neuron tuning properties alone because all responses were drawn from the same
55   distributions of single neuron responses. Instead, the population formatting arises from how responses to
56   motion and reward expectation are coordinated across the population.
57
58   Previous studies have found that the formatting of information from different sensory, cognitive, or motor
59   sources varies across brain areas, tasks, and species (18-27). Artificial networks that are trained to
60   perform many tasks or complex tasks tend to self-organize into modules that likely format information
61   differently (1, 2). The goal of our study was to use causal manipulations to link population formatting to
62   the role of a neuronal population in behavior.
63
64   New insights from a continuous, information-integration task
65   Our study hinged on having a behavioral task that 1) requires decision-makers to combine information
66   from multiple sources, so we could link population formatting to behavior, and 2) has a rich enough
67   behavioral output to test hypotheses about the relationship between neuronal responses, causal
68   manipulations, and behavior. We therefore trained monkeys to combine two information sources in a
69   continuous estimation task, which provides richer behavioral outputs when compared to typical binary
70   choice tasks (see also (30, 31)).
71
72   Two adult rhesus monkeys (Macaca mulatta, both male, 8 and 10 kg) combined sensory information
73   (motion direction) with cognitive information (the reward magnitude associated with different choices) to
74   make continuous choices. They indicated their choice on each trial by making an eye movement to a
75   target ring. The amount of fruit juice reward they earned depended on the accuracy of their motion
76   direction estimate and a scaling factor associated with particular choices. Colored portions of the target
77   ring indicated which choices would be associated with increased, decreased, or unchanged scaling of the
78   reward amount (Figure 1H, I, and Supplementary Figure 1). We refer to the center of the colored regions
79   as the increased reward or decreased reward center and different locations of those regions (which varied
80   from trial to trial) as reward conditions. During each session, the high reward center was one of two
81   opposite locations chosen randomly from trial to trial, and the low reward center was 180o opposite. We
82   chose the two locations based on the tuning of the recorded neurons, so there were different pairs of
83   locations during different sessions. The monkeys demonstrated that they integrated motion and reward
84   information by making choices that varied smoothly with the direction of the visual stimulus and biasing
85   choices toward larger expected rewards (Figure 1J, K; choices toward the high reward region were
86   typically associated with faster reaction times; Supplementary Figure 2).

133   Motion and reward information are formatted differently in visual and frontal cortex
134   While the monkeys performed the continuous estimation task, we recorded simultaneously from a few
135   dozen neurons in two brain areas thought to contribute differently to the decision computation (Figure 1I).
136   Neurons in area MT are critical for motion perception (8, 9, 31) and neurons in dlPFC are part of a
137   network involved in decision-making, reward processing, and eye movement planning (34-36).
138   Consistent with observations that sensory, cognitive, and motor information is encoded throughout the
139   brain (6, 7, 37, 38), motion and reward information were readily apparent in the population activity of
140   both MT and dlPFC (Figure 2) and dlPFC units tended to exhibit more non-linear mixed selectivity for
141   motion and reward than MT units ((17, 39); Supplementary Figure 3).
142
143   However, motion and reward information were formatted differently in the two areas. Motion information
144   in both areas was represented in a low-dimensional manner (Supplementary Figure 4). However, in MT,
145   population representations of motion and reward tended to be separable. This means that information
146   about them occupied distinct dimensions of a neuronal population space where each dimension represents
147   the response of one neuron (Figure 2A). In contrast, in dlPFC, motion and reward tended to be
148   represented jointly, such that they occupied overlapping dimensions in neuronal population space (Figure
149   2B). This difference in neuronal population formatting (Figure 2C and 2D) means that motion and reward
150   information is encoded independently in MT, while in dlPFC, the same population activity can indicate
151   different motion directions in different reward conditions. These differences in formatting make this a
152   good system in which to investigate questions about how formatting is related to the contribution of a
153   brain area to behavior.

192   An artificial neural network to generate causal hypotheses
193   We sought to use causal manipulations (e.g. measuring the impact of perturbing neuronal activity on
194   behavior) to understand the functional implications of differently formatted population representations.
195   From the existing literature, it was difficult to generate hypotheses about how the outcomes of causal
196   experiments would relate to information formatting. Some previous studies have attempted to link
197   neuronal population formatting to information coding or theoretical ideas about how that information
198   could, in principle, be read out (40-42). However, to our knowledge, no studies have linked neuronal
199   population formatting directly to behavior by comparing, in either model or experiment, the effect of
200   causally manipulating brain areas with different formatting.
201
202   To generate hypotheses that we could test in further causal experiments, we constructed a recurrent neural
203   network (RNN) that qualitatively reproduced the neuronal population formatting in MT and dlPFC
204   (Figure 3A). Our goal was to create a model that could make predictions about the impact of perturbing
205   neuronal activity in MT and dlPFC on behavior. Our model therefore took as inputs the same information
206   available to the monkey (motion and reward information). We first trained an MT-like module to receive
207   a noisy motion signal and the locations of increased and decreased reward centers as inputs and to output
208   sharpened estimates of stimulus motion direction and the reward center locations, independently. After
209   training, we froze the recurrent connections and input the motion output units of the MT-like module and
210   the reward center locations to the dlPFC-like module. This module was trained to output an estimate of
211   the reward associated with each possible choice given the motion direction and reward condition. We
212   took the model’s choice to be the direction associated with the highest expected reward (Figure 3B).
213
214   The goal of the model was not to provide a quantitatively precise account of the neural data, but to
215   capture key qualitative features of population formatting observed in MT and dlPFC and to generate
216   testable predictions about the effects of perturbing those populations. Consistent with the data, the model
217   represents motion and reward information in a relatively low-dimensional manner and differs across
218   modules in whether these sources of information are represented independently or jointly. Importantly,
219   these differences in formatting lead to distinct predictions about the effects of perturbations, which we
220   then tested experimentally. Like the monkeys, the model appropriately combined information about
221   motion and reward (qualitatively compare Figure 3B to Figure 1K). By construction, the recurrent layers
222   in the two modules represent motion and reward in ways qualitatively that mimic MT and dlPFC. Like
223   our population recordings, the model represented task information in a low-dimensional manner (see
224   Methods) and Demixed-PCA revealed that more variance is explained by the interaction of motion and
225   reward information in the dlPFC-module (20% of explained variance across conditions) than in the
226   MT module (0% variance explained across conditions; compare to Figure 2D). The MT-like module
227   represented motion and reward information separably (Figure 3C, D, E), while these features were
228   integrated in the dlPFC-like module (Figure 3F, G, H). This can be visualized by viewing the stable fixed
229   points which the activity of the hidden layer approaches at the end of a trial (one fixed point for each
230   combination of motion direction and the two reward conditions, Figure 3C, F; and, to more fully visualize
231   the population structure, across a larger range of possible reward conditions; Figure 3D, G ). The
232   similarities between the model and the data make this model a good tool for generating hypotheses about
233   the relationship between the population formatting and behavior. We note that the correspondence
234   between model and data is qualitative rather than quantitative, and we do not expect all features of the
235   measured population activity to be captured by this simplified model.

257   Perturbing units in the recurrent layers illuminates the different functional roles of the two modules
258   We used our models to generate causal hypotheses about how the impact of perturbing units in the
259   recurrent layer of each module (43) the way that module formats information (see Methods). Perturbing
260   units in the MT-like and dlPFC-like modules had qualitatively different effects on model choices.
261   Consistent with results from a previous study that electrically stimulated MT (31), activating the MT-like
262   module biased choices toward the preferred direction of the activated unit, provided that the difference
263   between the motion direction and the preference of the activated unit was less than approximately 150
264   (vector averaging; Figure 4A-C, column 1). Perturbing the dlPFC-like module was very different. On
265   some ostensibly identical trials, the model chose the motion direction and on others the preferred direction
266   of the activated unit, and essentially never in between (winner-take-all; Figure 4A-C, column 3). Further,
267   the behavioral impact of perturbation depended on the reward condition in the dlPFC-like module but not
268   the MT-like module (compare Figure 4B and Figure 4C).
269
270   Manipulating MT and dlPFC activity had qualitatively different effects on choices
271   We tested the predictions of our model by electrically microstimulating one electrode at a time in MT or
272   dlPFC (or neither) on randomly interleaved trials. Consistent with model predictions, microstimulating
273   MT and dlPFC affected choices in distinct ways. As in previous work (31) and as predicted by our model,
274   microstimulating MT biased choices midway between the visual stimulus motion direction and the
275   preferred direction measured at the stimulated site (vector averaging; Figure 4A-C, column 2). In contrast,
276   and consistent with the prediction of our model, microstimulating dlPFC produced a bimodal choice
277   distribution, where the monkeys sometimes reported the direction of the visual motion and other times
278   reported the preference measured at the stimulated site (winner-take-all; Figure 4A-C, column 4) with no
279   evidence of vector averaging. We note that the correspondence between model and data is qualitative
280   rather than quantitative, and we do not expect all features of the measured population activity to be
281   captured by this simplified model. The fact that the model reproduces the qualitative effects of
282   perturbations despite these differences suggests that the link between population formatting and
283   behavioral impact depends on coarse features of the representation rather than precise quantitative details.
284
285   Furthermore, and consistent with the predictions of our model, stimulating MT and dlPFC differently
286   interacted with the reward condition. The behavioral impact of MT microstimulation did not depend
287   strongly on whether the increased or decreased reward was aligned with the preference of the stimulated
288   cells (compare Figure 4B and 4C, column 2). The intuition is that because different information sources
289   are encoded approximately independently in MT, there is no interaction between the behavioral impact of
290   microstimulation and other information that impacts behavior, like reward expectation. In contrast, the
291   behavioral impact of dlPFC microstimulation is much greater when the increased reward center is aligned
292   with the preference of the stimulated cells (compare Figure 4B and 4C, column 4). This is consistent with
293   the observation that in dlPFC, the representations of motion and reward (and choices) are joint. It would
294   be impossible for stimulating those neurons to impact choices in a way that is independent of reward,
295   because no aspects of the representations are separable. Consistent with these intuitions, the impact of MT
296   microstimulation on choices was indistinguishable in the two conditions (Figure 5A), while dlPFC
297   microstimulation had a larger behavioral impact when the increased reward region was aligned with the
298   preference of the stimulated cells (Figure 5B). This difference between the areas did not depend on the
299   coherence of the visual motion stimulus (Supplementary Figure 7).

336   Interpreting population formatting in relation to task variables
337   An important issue in interpreting these results is the relationship between what information is
338   represented in a neuronal population and how that information is formatted. In our task, variables such as
339   motion direction, reward condition, and choice are inherently related, and activity in dlPFC in particular is
340   expected to reflect choice-related signals that depend on both motion and reward. Our goal was not to
341   assign exclusive representations of specific variables to each area or to fully dissociate these variables.
342   Instead, we asked whether the formatting of task-relevant information at the population level provides
343   insight into how different areas contribute to behavior.
344
345   A key is that differences in formatting make distinct predictions about the effects of causal perturbations.
346   When information sources are represented independently, perturbations should affect behavior in a
347   manner that is largely invariant to other task variables. In contrast, when information is represented
348   jointly, the effect of perturbations should depend on the state of those other variables. Consistent with this
349   prediction, the behavioral impact of MT microstimulation was largely independent of reward condition,
350   whereas the impact of dlPFC microstimulation depended strongly on reward alignment. This difference is
351   captured by the population formatting in each area and does not depend on whether dlPFC activity is
352   interpreted as reflecting motion, reward, or choice per se. Rather, it reflects how these sources of
353   information are organized within the population response.
354
355   Because our interpretation does not require a strict dissociation between motion, reward, and choice
356   signals, we performed an additional analysis to assess whether the difference in population formatting
357   across areas could be explained by choice-related activity alone (see Methods / Supplementary Figure 8).
358   These analyses confirm that dlPFC activity contains strong choice-related components, as expected, but
359   that the key differences in population formatting between areas persist beyond these components.
360
361   Interestingly, the requirement of our task that monkeys combine information from visual motion and
362   reward expectation may have heightened the difference between MT and dlPFC stimulation. Unlike
363   previous studies that elicited winner-take-all behavior using MT microstimulation when the angle
364   between the visual motion and the preference of stimulated site was large (31, 44), we typically saw
365   vector averaging whenever we stimulated MT in our main experiment. We hypothesize that the reward
366   scaling encouraged the monkeys to bias choices towards a larger reward even when the visual motion
367   evidence suggested a choice associated with a smaller reward. Indeed, when we ran a subset of
368   experiments with no reward scaling, we occasionally observed winner-take-all effects during MT
369   microstimulation when the difference between the direction of the visual motion and the preference of the
370   stimulated site was large (Supplementary Figure 9).
371
372   Two controls highlight the robustness of the different impact of MT and dlPFC microstimulation on
373   behavior. First, we investigated the possibility that the impact of stimulation was larger in one brain area
374   than the other, which led to qualitatively different impact on behavior. We controlled for this by changing
375   the relative strength of the visual and electrical stimuli by reducing the visual motion coherence. Rather
376   than qualitatively changing the impact of MT or dlPFC stimulation, this manipulation simply enhanced
377   the impact of microstimulation in both areas, more strongly biasing choices towards the preference of the
378   stimulated site in MT and increasing the proportion of trials on which the monkey reported the preference
379   of the stimulated site in dlPFC. The qualitative difference between the two areas remained even at low
380   motion coherence (Supplementary Figure 10 and 11).
381
382   Second, we investigated the possibility that the different impact of stimulation in the two areas was
383   attributable to the fact that for most experiments, the visual motion stimulus overlapped the MT receptive
384   fields and the target ring overlapped the response fields of the dlPFC neurons. We controlled for this
385   difference by conducting a subset of microstimulation experiments when the MT receptive fields
386   overlapped the target ring rather than the visual motion stimulus. In these experiments, we did not
387   systematically bias choices or induce winner-take-all behavior (Supplementary Figure 12).

389   A causal link between neuronal population formatting and function
390
391   Our study made a leap from correlation to causation in the understanding of the relationship between the
392   formatting of neuronal population responses and behavior. As in previous studies (44-48), we found that
393   when choices are based on combining motion and reward information, both MT and dlPFC encode both
394   information sources. However, we demonstrated that those representations are formatted differently. This
395   observation means that this system is a platform for testing predictions about what neuronal population
396   formatting implies about the function of those areas.
397
398   Electrical microstimulation has long been used as a tool to understand the contribution of a population of
399   neurons to behavior and there is a well-known relationship between the effects of microstimulation on
400   behavior and the spatial organization of feature tuning in cortex (49). MT clearly contains more spatial
401   organization for motion direction information than dlPFC, but the relationship between spatial
402   organization and population formatting is not obvious and is an important avenue for future study.
403
404   A growing body of theoretical work has quantified how flexibility or robustness in the way that neuronal
405   population representations could, in principle, guide behavior depends on population formatting (23, 50-
406   52). Indeed, this theoretical framework predicts that the formatting differences between MT and dlPFC
407   are consistent with longstanding ideas that visual cortex functions as a close to veridical representation of
408   the world and that dlPFC flexibly combines information to guide decision-making (34, 35, 47). However,
409   we showed using our model trained only to reproduce the formatting that there is a causal link between
410   formatting and function: as predicted by our models, electrically stimulating those areas had a
411   qualitatively different impact on behavior. Our results support the exciting idea that the contributions of
412   less well-studied brain areas to less well-studied behaviors can in the future be understood by measuring
413   the way they format information from different sources.
414
415   We used artificial neural networks in a slightly different way than many recent neuroscience studies (2,
416   50, 53-60), because we used them simply as hypothesis generators. The use of RNNs has generated some
417   controversy in neuroscience, mainly out of concern over the fact that the architecture of these networks is
418   vastly different from biological networks (61). Our work highlights the value of using recurrent neural
419   network models that are constrained by neuronal data to generate experimentally testable predictions
420   about the relationships between neurons and behavior rather than as models of a biophysically realistic
421   mechanism.
422
423   Finally, our study provides a resolution to the seeming paradox of having multiple brain areas that encode
424   redundant sensory, cognitive, and motor information (‘everything is everywhere’). Our results suggest
425   that different brain areas divide up neuronal computations in ways that are reflected in the formatting of
426   different information sources. We demonstrate that the ability to decode information from a neuronal
427   population is not sufficient to determine how that population contributes to behavior. Instead, future
428   efforts to understand neuronal computations and to intervene in disorders that affect cognition should
429   focus on how the flexible reformatting of neuronal population responses in different areas enables flexible
430   behavior.
