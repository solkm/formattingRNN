46   1. Population formatting simulation
47   We conducted a simple simulation to demonstrate that it is possible for populations of neurons with
48   identical visual tuning functions and reward modulation to format information in different ways. We
49   simulated two populations of neurons, each of which consisted of units with the same motion direction
50   tuning functions, which were Von Mises functions with uniformly distributed preferred directions
51   (preferred directions varied in steps of 10).
52
53   We simulated modulation by reward expectation in ways consistent with the small, heterogeneous,
54   multiplicative gain changes observed in neuronal responses in tasks that vary reward expectation and
55   feature attention (1, 2). We drew gains for each neuron from a uniform distribution between 1 and 1.2 and
56   used these as a multiplier to the response of each neuron. Inspired by single neuron studies, we scaled this
57   multiplier by the difference between the preferred direction of the neuron and the center of the high
58   reward region. Depending on the extent to which these random gains happened to coordinate across the
59   population, we could choose random draws such that the population formatted motion and reward
60   information differently (Figure 1A-G).
61
62   2. Subjects
63   We trained two adult male rhesus monkeys, S and O (8 and 10 kg, respectively) to perform a continuous
64   motion estimation task with variable reward expectation. Before training, both monkeys were prepared
65   surgically with a custom titanium head holding device. To enable electrophysiological recordings, two
66   recording cylinders (Crist instruments Co., Inc., Hagerstown, MD) were placed over 1) the principal
67   sulcus and 2) visual cortex to provide access to dlPFC and MT, respectively. All surgical and behavioral
68   procedures conformed to the guidelines established by the National Institutes of Health and were
69   approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees of the University of Pittsburgh and
70   Carnegie Mellon.
71
72   3. Behavioral task
73
74   3.1. Procedures
75   During training and recording sessions, the monkeys sat in a primate chair that provided head restraint.
76   Visual stimuli were presented at a 120Hz refresh rate on a high-quality LCD monitor (VPixx
77   Technologies Inc., QC Canada) placed 57cm from the monkey’s eyes. Eye movements were monitored
78   using an infrared optical eye tracker (Eyelink 1000, SR Research, ON, Canada). Behavioral control and
79   stimulus presentation were managed by computers using a Unix based operating system (running
80   MATLAB, The MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA, and PsychToolbox (3, 4)).
81
82   3.2. Task and training
83
84   A trial began when the monkey fixated a central spot on a black background (inside an invisible 1
85   diameter window). Then a circular target ring was displayed and remained visible for the remainder of the
86   trial. The target ring was uniform gray except for two segments 180 apart that were colored either pink
87   or purple to signify increased or decreased reward scaling (see below and in Supplementary Figure 1).
88   After a 200-400 ms delay, a dynamic random dot kinematogram was displayed in a stationary circular
89   aperture inside the target ring. These motion stimuli were identical to those described in (5) and were
90   presented at a single motion strength (coherence), typically between 40 and 50%, for most experiments
91   (exceptions are described in Supplementary Figures 10 and 11). During multi-coherence experiments,
92   trials using two motion coherences were randomly interleaved (low coherence: 15% - 20%; high
93   coherence: 35% - 50%) and during the experiments described in Supplementary Figure 9 where there was
94   no reward scaling on the target ring, data from only low coherence trials (15% - 20%) are shown.
95
 96   Monkeys were trained to make a saccadic eye movement to a location on the target ring to signify their
 97   choice. The monkeys could initiate the eye movement between 150 and 1500 ms after visual stimulus
 98   onset. The reward was based on the accuracy of the motion estimate, scaled by the amount indicated by
 99   the location of the pink- and purple- colored portions of the target ring. Which color signified the
100   increased or decreased reward center switched in blocks with randomized, unsignaled changes throughout
101   the experiment (typically 2-8 changes per session; Supplementary Figure 1).
102
103   During training, the monkeys first learned to perform the motion continuous estimation task before the
104   reward scaling was introduced. The monkeys learned to saccade to a location on the target ring that
105   matched the direction of motion of the visual stimulus, not where the vector of motion of the stimulus
106   would cross the target ring. This design ensured that the monkeys’ judgements would not be biased by the
107   exact location of either the visual motion stimulus or the target ring, both of which could vary based on
108   the receptive field locations of the recorded neurons.

111   4. Electrophysiology experiments
112
113   4.1. Procedures
114
115   Neurophysiological recordings were performed using 24 or 32 channel linear probes (V- and S- probes;
116   Plexon Inc, Dallas, TX) positioned using grids (Crist Instruments Company Inc., Hagerstown, MD) and
117   advanced using a hydraulic microdrive (Kopf instruments, Tujunga, CA). Spiking activity, local field
118   potentials, eye position, and task events were recorded at 30,000 samples/second (using Trellis software
119   and Ripple recording hardware; Ripple, Salt Lake City, UT). All data were analyzed using custom scripts
120   (MATLAB; The MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA).
121
122   4.2. Recording locations
123
124   Recordings in MT were made via a chamber positioned over visual cortex aimed anteriorly toward the
125   middle temporal lobe. This posterior approach provided access to groups of MT units with similar spatial
126   receptive fields but different direction tuning preferences across the contacts of a multi-channel probe (6,
127   7). MT units were identified based on stereotactic coordinates, gray-white matter transitions, and
128   functional properties. In most experiments, we aimed for MT units with receptive fields that were less
129   than 12 degrees eccentric so we could place the motion stimulus within their receptive fields without
130   overlapping the target ring. When we electrically stimulated MT neurons whose receptive fields
131   overlapped the target ring (Supplementary Figure 12), we used neurons with more eccentric receptive
132   fields. During all recordings, we advanced the probe until we could stably record from as many visually
133   responsive and direction-tuned units as possible. We measured spatial receptive field and direction tuning
134   to verify electrode positioning, guide motion stimulus positioning, and, when relevant, identify the tuning
135   preferences of sites recorded on the contacts we used for electrical stimulation.
136
137   Recordings in dlPFC were made via a chamber positioned over frontal cortex, anterior to the arcuate
138   sulcus and spanning both sides of the principal sulcus (determined by stereotactic location and visual
139   inspection during surgery). We recorded units along the banks and gyri of the principal sulcus. We used
140   recording locations in which we observed spatial selectivity during any period of a delayed saccade task,
141   and we stopped advancing the probe when we could stably record from as many selective units as
142   possible. We typically recorded from units with response fields at similar eccentricities, whose locations
143   sometimes progressed counter-clockwise as penetration depth increased. We preferred units whose
144   response fields were more than 10 degrees eccentric so we could position a portion of the target ring
145   within them and avoid overlap with the visual motion stimulus. We placed one colored portion of the ring
146   at a location that was well covered by the response fields of the recorded units, and the other colored
147   region was placed 180 away.
148
149   4.3. Electrical microstimulation
150
151   During electrical microstimulation experiments, we selected two contacts on the probe to be stimulated on
152   randomly interleaved trials. Each trial consisted either of no microstimulation or stimulation on one
153   electrode, and there was no statistical relationship between stimulation and the motion stimulus or reward
154   condition. Microstimulation experiments were only ever performed in one area per session. Stimulation
155   electrodes were randomly selected from suitable candidates based on tuning curves measured at the start
156   of an experiment. MT sites were required to have a suitable visual receptive field location and strong
157   direction tuning. dlPFC sites were required to have suitably positioned visual or premotor response fields.
158
159   Electrical microstimulation began with the onset of the visual motion stimulus and remained on until the
160   monkey moved its eyes out of the fixation window to initiate a saccade. Microstimulation was biphasic
161   and delivered at 200 Hz with an amplitude that ranged across sessions between 20 and 40 A (8-10).
162
163   4.4 Quantifying the behavioral impact of microstimulation
164
165   To investigate the interaction between reward expectation and the impact of microstimulation on choices,
166   we summarized the impact of microstimulation in each reward and stimulation condition (Figure 5).
167   Because MT stimulation biased choices, we summarized behavior as the slope of the line relating the
168   choices to the direction of the visual motion stimulus (restricted to visual motion stimuli that were within
169   -120 degrees of the center of the region of increased reward scaling). We summarized the stimulation-
170   related change as the change in slope between trials with and without stimulation. Because dlPFC
171   microstimulation produced winner take all choices, we summarized the stimulation-related change in
172   behavior as the difference in the proportion of choices that were aligned with the preference of the
173   stimulated cells (defined as choices within 10 degrees of the preference of the stimulated site.
174
175   5. Analysis of electrophysiological data
176
177   5.1. Inclusion criteria
178
179   MT units were included for analysis if their average firing rate during a 250 ms epoch following visual
180   motion onset, lagged 50 ms for response latency, was at least 10% higher than during a period of stable
181   fixation on a blank screen. dlPFC units were included for analysis if their firing rate during either of the
182   two 250 ms epochs that followed the onset of the target ring or the visual motion stimulus, lagged by 50
183   ms, or the 250 ms that preceded eye movements was at least 10% higher than during a period of stable
184   fixation on a blank screen. Trials were included for analysis if the monkey indicated a choice by looking
185   at the target ring between 150 ms and 1500 ms after visual motion onset. Spike counts from a 300 ms
186   window, aligned to visual stimulus onset and shifted 50ms for latency, were used for subsequent analyses.
187
188   5.2. Mixed selectivity analysis
189
190   We analyzed non-linear mixed selectivity for recordings from 15 sessions in which we recorded MT and
191   dlPFC simultaneously. We evaluated the extent to which a linear scaling of motion direction tuning
192   curves from one reward condition could explain the tuning curve in the other reward condition. We
193   normalized the two vectors of mean responses from one unit from each reward condition such that their
194   combined mean equaled 1. Next, we fit a line that scaled the vector from one condition to responses from
195   the other and we evaluated the mean squared error in Supplementary Figure 3.
196
197   5.3. Dimensionality reduction and angle calculations
198
199   To visualize the structure of population activity in monkey (Figure 2) and model (Figure 3), we
200   performed principal components analysis on trial averaged responses that were either marginalized across
201   visual motion direction or reward conditions. We quantified the extent to which information about the two
202   task features was represented in the same dimensions of neuronal population activity, as the angle
203   between the first principal components for visual motion direction and reward (Figure 2C). Additionally,
204   we investigated neuronal population formatting using de-mixed principal components analysis (11) with
205   marginalizations for motion direction, reward condition, time, and the combination of motion direction
206   and reward (Figure 2D).
207
208   5.4 Regression, residual and decoding analysis
209   We analyzed the amount of variance explained by different task variables by performing linear regression
210   on responses of individual units from 15 recording sessions in which we recorded MT and dlPFC
211   simultaneously. A full regression model that included stimulus motion direction, reward condition and
212   choice was compared to a model that only included choice (Supplementary Figure 8A). Residuals from
213   the choice only model were then used, alongside the full data, in a decoding analysis to assess the amount
214   of information in MT and dlPFC populations that was separate from choice-related activity. For stimulus
215   motion decoding (Supplementary Figure 8B-C) the motion direction of the visual stimulus was predicted
216   on individual trials using leave-one-out regression and the full list of predicted directions was correlated
217   with the actual shown directions. For reward condition decoding (Supplementary Figure 8D-E) the reward
218   condition was predicted on individual trials using leave-one-out regression and the full list of predicted
219   reward conditions was correlated with the actual reward condition. Each decoding analysis was performed
220   separately for the full dataset and the choice-removed residuals.

268   In the MT-like module, the first two dimensions of activity representing direction information accounted
269   for 98.0% of the response variance and the first reward dimension accounted for 55.3% of the variance
270   explained across the possible reward conditions (tested in steps of 20 degrees from 0 to 340). In the dlPFC-
271   like module, activity is higher dimensional and the first two dimensions of activity representing direction
272   information accounted for 52.2% of the response variance across stimulus motion conditions and the first
273   reward dimension accounted for 51.5% of the variance across the possible reward conditions.

275   6.2 Model unit perturbation
276   To generate predictions about the impact of differences between the information representation in the MT-
277   like and dlPFC-like module, we performed perturbation experiments in the fully trained models. We
278   perturbed the activity of individual units in the recurrent layers of each module by adding an additional
279   untrained input (that projected only to the stimulated unit) during the motion input period.
